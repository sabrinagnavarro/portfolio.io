The Digital Humanities and “Old” and “New Media”

Sabrina Navarro · Aug 31, 2024

I study media at Mercer University. In media studies, we learn about how media affects society and how it actively changes, influences, and constructs society. Media means medium and is related to technologies that spread content. Examples include radio, newspapers, books, television, and the internet. Something I find interesting is the concept of old and new media. “New media” includes the internet and its higher reach and activity by its readers. I was drawn to the Public Writing and Digital Humanities course because to understand how media can construct and influence society, I must first understand how writers, or content creators, can connect with their audiences through these technologies.

With the internet and social media being relatively new technological advancements, it is interesting to see how much they have changed communication. With the inclusion of books, movies, informational videos, and newspapers online, individuals who could not gain access to these resources before can find them easier. In this sense, this “new media” has changed the world, education-wise, but with the inclusion of social media, it changes how the world communicates. Communication also has easier and wider accessibility through the Internet. Communities can come together on a topic and discuss interests with a wider range of people from all around the world. Discovering literature, art, language, music, etc., can take many different paths through the internet.

It becomes a kind of performance for content creators to share their art on the internet. With “old media,” like physical books or newspapers, the artists themselves did not need to connect with their audience in any other way than the art that they were making. With “new media,” and social media, the artists themselves become the art. Their personality or persona on the internet becomes just as important as the product of their content. Concerning what it means to be human, I think that in some ways “new media” almost prioritizes the human and the creator more than their content, especially in relation to music, influencers, or other social artists. However, with the introduction of artificial intelligence, I think the centralization around humanity may be thrown off. My goal is to learn more about the digital humanities to understand the relationship built between humans and technology, and how these technologies may change what it means to be human.

I Am Not A Robot

Sabrina Navarro · Sep 28, 2024

As a student in digital humanities, I’ve been assigned a paper that asks about what makes a text essentially human. This is in reference to artificial intelligence, as is the emergence of machine learning programs like ChatGPT. The assignment asks to use a literary text to support my claim, for which I will be using Wagoner’s “The Author of American Ornithology Sketches a Bird, Now Extinct.” The poem tells a story about a man who shot down a bird so he could draw and study it. By the end of the poem, the bird dies and the artist feels regret for effectively ending its life. This poem brought up a lot of questions in the class, that I took an interest in (“Is it moral to kill a bird in order for it to live on forever in a sketchbook?”). Using this text along with the ideas from Noam Chomsky in “The False Promise of Chat GPT,” which discusses the immediate differences between human thought and A.I. outcome, I can write out possible ideas for the in-class essay.

Chomsky argues that artificial intelligence is unable to explain or create meaning. He uses examples like asking ChatGPT about morality to support his argument. Morality, explanation, and thinking are what makes humans different from artificial intelligence. It’s this idea that can help me use Wagoner’s poem as an argument. The morality issue within Wagoner’s poem, whether it was moral or not to kill the bird, can be asked to ChatGPT and it gives a similar response to Chomsky’s questions asked on morality within his article. Will ChatGPT be able to give an ethical, moral explanation of the difficulty of the question asked?
