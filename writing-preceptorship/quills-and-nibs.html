Writing Preceptorship

Quills and Nibs Collage

Quills and Nibs were mini-essays that asked questions about how I write. More specifically what I do or think about as I go through certain steps in the writing process, such as pre-writing, drafting, paraphrasing, transitions, etc.

  • Academic writing fulfills a space that I think I would otherwise not have in the classroom or for myself. I find it hard to articulate my thoughts both in and outside the classroom. As a result, I tend to stay to myself and not speak up in class lectures because I need time to fully process the ideas and themes of the class. Academic writing allows me to sit down and face my thoughts and create meaning out of them. It is a way for me to connect with the professor and with myself. My academic audience includes my professors but also my future self. Many of the classes I take can be related to each other and sometimes my ideas from a past class can aid me in understanding something new.

    Academic writing has helped me understand that writing is an art. I remember dreading the thought of another paper or discussion post until I started writing for Dr. Black. He has a lot of writing rules that sound tedious at first but as you write it becomes more apparent why he wanted his students to use them. Following his rules led me to create my own, and with this, I understood the writing voice that everyone has. Even if a writer is writing about the same thing following the same rules, they never end up writing the same thing, their voices are different. This voice is similar to a hand that paints or draws. It can create something unique to the person, unique to their thoughts.

    As a preceptor, I would explain to other students that learning how to write effectively doesn’t just help academically but also for any career in the future. Being able to write effectively allows for easier communication of their thoughts whether that be in an academic or personal setting. It shows intelligence just how someone is able to construct an argument and organize their ideas. It is not just something to get better at to have good grades in classes or appease job interviewers. Writing effectively helps individuals find how they want their voices to be shared in a way that allows for the broad or general audience to understand.

  • Good essays come from a well-constructed argument and strong, supportive evidence. To have these, I believe it’s important to research, outline, and pre-write. It’s important to start with these steps rather than disregard them and dive straight into the essay. Essays that aren’t properly researched lack sufficient support for their claim and may end up straying away from their point. Something similar may occur when an individual doesn’t outline or structure their essay before they write it. After talking to a couple of students, and being one myself, I noticed that many of them dread the pre-writing stage. Many of them skip right into a rough draft rather than plan an outline. Although this may work for some of them, I find that without proper planning, the essay may struggle with an underdeveloped thesis, improper paragraph flow, or lack of quality evidence. I think good essays come with proper structure and care for the topic, which may only be done through research and pre-writing.

    My writing process either starts with research or writing an outline. This depends on whether or not I know what my argument is. Let’s say I know exactly what my argument will be before I start writing. I would write up a simple outline that structured all the main points I wanted to hit in a particular, logical, paragraph order. After that, I would start finding articles or examples that supported my position. Then, there would be a lot of back-and-forth on research and planning as something may come up in my research that I want to add to my argument. Once I have a sufficient amount of research, I will set up my essay using the writing style that I’ve chosen and write brief “topic” sentences for the different paragraphs I want to include. These sentences are just to help get the big idea for those paragraphs out, I will later bullet point my supporting evidence that I’d want to use in those spaces. Finally, after I finish my pre-writing, I will start writing the rough draft. Sometimes my thesis statement won’t be fully fleshed out until my essay is finished since my argument and ideas develop as I write the paper. My writing process is specific to me, however, I believe that pre-writing in any sense is still important to do before writing a good academic paper.

  • Structure is an important factor in good writing. How one is able to set up their ideas and maintain a logical flow, determines how well a reader may be able to understand an argument and follow it. A disorganized paragraph or argument structure may leave the reader confused about what the argument is and how the evidence supports it. With my experience as a preceptor, some papers I have come across lack structuring and flow between paragraphs. Similarly, papers lacking a well-structured argument leave me confused about the overall goal and intention of the student's words. I think an important part of a well-structured paper starts with a strong introductory paragraph (this includes a good topic sentence and thesis statement). This paragraph sets up the entire paper. The introductory paragraph can set the flow of the paper through its introduction of ideas. If the paper follows the same flow as the introduction it is easy to read and understand the ideas being brought forth.

    As a writer, I build my individual ideas into larger components through outlines, my introductory paragraph, and my transitions between paragraphs. Rather than starting with an argument and splitting my argument into explanation and evidence, I will have evidence that I want to use and from there I use the evidence to create an argument. Annotation is very important to my process because as I write my thoughts down, I can find a link or a pattern within my thoughts that can start to form an argument or an outline. As mentioned before, my introductory paragraph helps me set up the rest of my paragraph (in reality it is more my outline that does this, but I tend to replicate the flow of ideas from within my introductory paragraph). I also prefer to link my paragraph's ideas together. By this, I mean that my last sentence within each paragraph will usually hint at, or tie into, what the next paragraph will discuss. My arguments are structured in a similar but smaller way. My thesis statement explains my claim and evidence very briefly so that I am able to expand on each point throughout the paper. Structuring my papers is important for both me as the writer and the reader. By structuring it correctly it helps me connect my ideas together in a way I can understand and by doing this I am able to help the reader understand where I am connecting those ideas.

  • Writers intentionally use the raw materials to construct an essay through a clear thesis statement, picking out the most impactful and supportive evidence, and a detailed outline. Technically, the creation of an outline is a part of the pre-writing process, however, the outline is important for the construction of an essay. The outline is the paper before it is formally written. The big decisions of what the argument is, what supporting evidence should be highlighted more, and what order/structure the paper should have, depend on how the outline was written. In writing the first draft, it is important to follow a flow of ideas that lead directly to an answer to one’s thesis statement.

    When I draft an essay, I tend to skip the rough draft and go straight to the final draft. Once I have all the raw materials I need to build an outline, I take my time to flesh out a detailed outline that almost replicates a rough draft. I put everything I want to talk about in separate sections and add whatever quotes or evidence I want to use for that paragraph under that section so all I need to do when I am finished with the outline is fill it in with words. Once that draft is finished, I go back and edit or have someone else edit the paper to see where I could fix some mistakes. I have a tendency to overthink how I want to phrase a sentence and having an outline helps me stay on track and see exactly where I am headed, which helps me understand where I want a sentence to go. Drafting plays a big role in my work as an academic writer, especially for large papers that may feel overwhelming. Many of my professors have responses or small papers due every week which helps me practice writing outlines and drafting throughout the academic semester. Within my own outlines, I write a fully detailed thesis statement and have all my important evidence written down and separated into paragraphs (or split into multiple if a more complex subject), so constructing an essay and drafting flows smoothly.

  • When I write, I make connections through the ideas. I create a very brief, light, connection between my thesis and evidence in the introduction of the essay. Structurally, I put ideas that can lead to each other in a specific paragraph order (this is also somewhat replicated in the introduction). I use a topic sentence to introduce the idea within the paragraph (and how it relates to my thesis statement). My closing statement briefly leads into the next paragraph or idea. I believe that transitions are important for the flow of a paper and that it could sound very choppy and disorganized otherwise.

    As a writing preceptor, my goal is to polish and edit the students’ work. With past assignments, their biggest issues have been with creating arguments and topic sentences, so we have been working on fixing those larger issues within their papers. Only recently have I seen a dramatic shift in their writing. Their arguments have become clearer and topic sentences in paragraphs are being used correctly. With this improvement, I have been able to help them with smaller mistakes. The most apparent mistake, that has been consistent with almost every student, has been their transitions.

    I notice a lack of transition both as a reader and as a writer. Although, yes, it is more noticeable as a reader to catch problems with transitions in papers, after reading so many papers and writing so many outlines, I can notice those issues within my own papers. A problem I face as a writer is noticing the problems my paper has, and not having enough time to fix them. Unfortunately, transitions are something I tend to place on the back burner and deem not as important as other problems I may see. However, after reading some of the papers in my class, I’ve realized that the lack of transition between paragraphs is very noticeable within a paper and affects the way an argument is presented. By editing their papers and seeing the importance of transitions, I hope to hold transitions to a higher priority.
