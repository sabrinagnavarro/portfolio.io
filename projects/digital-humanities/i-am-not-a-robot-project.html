Digital Humanities

I Am Not A Robot Project

The I Am Not A Robot Project prompted me to answer the question “What makes writing essentially human?” To aid in answering this question, I used two different texts and analyzed both together to help support my argument. I was meant to turn in an open letter, analog essay, and annotations of the answer that ChatGPT had to this prompt.

  • Dear Fellow Writers,

    As technology advances and A.I. machine learning systems continue to grow, the writers' future (and other artists alike) may have their voices stolen and replicated through these systems. In my class on Digital Humanities, I had a project called “I Am Not a Robot” which asked the question of what makes writing essentially “human?” To answer this question, I examined “The False Promise of ChatGPT” by Noam Chomsky and “The Author of American Ornithology Sketches a Bird, Now Extinct” by David Wagoner.

    ChatGPT may help students when time has run out to work on an assignment or when they are trying to understand academic writing that is far too advanced for their brains to comprehend. But what does this mean for the future of student learning?

    Will ChatGPT be able to authentically replicate our voices? I don’t think so. A computer can’t create beauty, or like humans, make beauty within their failures. After playing around with the system, ChatGPT gives Uncanny Valley with its writing. It’s almost as if there was a human or a piece of humanity within its writing but then something is off. It’s almost as if I was attempting to draw a self-portrait of myself on paper, it resembles a human but it’s not fooling anyone.

    Before starting this project, I will admit I did use some form of A.I. in brainstorming for a paper during my sophomore year. I was in a similar place that I believe a lot of students find themselves in, confusion. Hoping to gain some more understanding on my paper, I used A.I. to help me figure out the topic I wanted to write about. I would say that it helped me gain a better understanding of the paper, however, I never ended up using the system again as I found that using the texts provided by my classes helped me understand more…shocker. A.I. just uses data it finds anywhere on the internet, which is not a very reliable source I’d say.

    While reading David Wagoner’s poem, I realized the implications it had when compared to the prompt of what makes writing human. In my analog essay, I argued that A.I. can never replicate human writing because of the lack of authenticity, ethics, morals, and intended goals. Wagoner’s poem explores how an artist comes across a bird, and kills it in an attempt to “make it live forever in books.” By the end of the poem, the artist feels regret for killing the bird. His sketch of the bird will never be able to “live up to” the bird’s actual life. The authenticity is lost.

    When I plugged in the prompt of the project into ChatGPT, the A.I. misunderstood the true meaning of the poem, and instead took the poem by its words, literally. In one of its sections, ChatGPT explained how the cultural and historical contexts of both texts explain that A.I. is not able to see the impact of technology and the historical significance of birds. I found this answer quite interesting since I’m asking a question that would require some insight on the impact of technology and the poem has nothing to do with the historical significance of birds. It uses the words by Wagoner and Chomsky in their literal contexts rather than their deeper meanings and intentions. By doing this A.I. kind of misses the whole point and proves its point.

    In another answer ChatGPT gave me, it said that it couldn’t replicate human voice through writing, however, it can try and seem to be able to match popular artists' patterns in their voice. I asked it to answer the questions again as if they were Taylor Swift and again as if they were William Shakespeare to see what it could come up with. The responses were hilarious in that, yes it replicated those artists’ however, like I mentioned before, something was definitely off.

    For now, A.I. cannot replicate “human” nature, beauty, failures, morals, empathy, and many other things authentically. It has no intention other than to spill data, that the user wants, from the internet. But what happens when in ten or so years from now, A.I. advances and “learns” quicker, better, and more accurately than ever before? Could students be able to put their own work in and ask them to write something that matches their own voice? Maybe. I still believe they will be wrong in many cases, especially when a prompt or any kind of creativity needs to be produced. But what if they could make A.I. create its own voice, does that defeat its own purpose? Does A.I. need to be restrictive in order to please all users?

    These are questions for a different paper that I am not writing. For what A.I. is at the moment, it does nothing for its liberal arts users, so don’t use it. I mean you can use it, everyone has free will to an extent, however, I would personally not recommend it.

    Sincerely,
    Sabrina Navarro

  • With the introduction to A.I. and machine learning technologies, the question of what makes writing essentially human gets brought up. In Noam Chomsky’s “The False Promise of ChatGPT,” Chomsky argues that A.I. will never be able to replicate human writing as A.I. programs, like ChatGPT, struggle with human explanation and molarity. In David Wagoner’s “The Author of American Ornithology Sketches a Bird, Now Extinct,” Wagnoer brings up issues of morality. The artist kills a bird in order to preserve its memory in a sketchbook, but later regrets their decision. Using both texts, what makes a piece of writing essentially “human” is authenticity of an intended goal.

    Chomsky argues that machine learning systems cannot replicate the human mind as it lacks the capacity “...of explanation, the mark of true intelligence” (Chomsky 2) and is also not “...capable of moral thinking” (Chomsky 4). A.I. writing takes data, finds patterns, and generates probable outputs intended to replicate authentic human writing (Chomsky 2). However, this is flawed as A.I. is only able to describe and predict, but unable to see “...what is not the case and what could not be the case” (Chomsky 2). Similarly, A.I. does not contain human ethics and morals. Humans are able to balance creativity with constraint while ChatGPT will struggle to balance in between. With Chomsky’s experiment, he learned that ChatGPT will “...either overgenerate (producing both truths and falsehoods, endorsing ethical and unethical decisions alike) or undergenerate (exhibiting noncommitment to any decisions and indifference to consequences)” (Chomsky 8). With these two factors in mind, A.I. will never be able to produce authentic, human-replicated content. Identically, in Wagoner’s poem, the true authenticity of another being can never be achieved.

    Wagoner’s poem describes a scene of a bird being shot in order to “...make them live forever inside books” (Wagoner, line 15). This action in itself brings up questions of morality. Should one kill a living being to prolong its life within a book forever? Or does the act of taking something’s life away from nature and the creation of experience, take away the beauty that was meant to be replicated? By the end of the poem, the artist is filled with, the very human emotion, of regret. In the last line, Wagoner says “He watched it die, he said, with great regret” (Wagoner, line 31). What exactly the artist was regretful about is not stated but can be inferred. The artist came to a realization that the beauty of the bird, he was so drawn to, could not be replicated through his art. No matter how perfect and “down to the feather” he was in his sketch, the bird’s life, beauty, and authenticity could not be forged through another means other than its individualist self.

    Both texts discover the beauty within authenticity. The dead bird, drawn in a sketchbook, will never be the bird living within nature. The artist saw this and lives with the regret of his action, even with his intention to preserve the bird “forever.” Chomsky’s argument on A.I. and its inability to replicate human explanation and morality demonstrates the same ideal. A.I. words come from a collection of human data, only searching for patterns and probable answers, and therefore will never be able to replicate, authentic, human writing in the same way the artist cannot replicate the beauty and life of the bird.

    The goal of A.I. is to use technology to conceptualize human language and knowledge while the goal of the artist in Wagoner’s poem was to forcibly gather artistic information on a bird. Both intended goals were not met due to the failure to produce authenticity.
