Digital Humanities

The Mercer Writers’ Project

As the final project for the Digital Humanities course, I was prompted to write a Mercer Story. My story is about Reg Murphy, a past Mercer student who inspired the name for the Reg Murphy Center of Collaborative Journalism. I chose this story because of his recent passing (November 9, 2024) and because I find his story to be inspirational to any Journalism or Media Studies student at Mercer.

Reg Murphy and the Media Studies Department

Also known as the CCJ, the Reg Murphy Center of Collaborative Journalism is a collaborative space that combines both the Journalism and Media Studies departments on Mercer’s Campus. Here is what Mercer’s website has to state about Reg Murphy:

“In 2024, the Center was named for distinguished alumnus, an accomplished journalist, and longtime publishing executive J. Reg Murphy, who began his journalism career while an undergraduate student at Mercer in the 1950s. He covered local news, sports, and the state capitol for the Macon Telegraph before going on to serve as a reporter for, and then editor of, the Atlanta Constitution…He served five terms on the Mercer Board of Trustees, was named a Distinguished Alumnus in 1971, and was awarded an honorary degree from the University in 1975.”

The building itself was built in 2011, using $6 million in grants from different foundations.

Reg Murphy actually dropped out of Mercer University to progress his career in journalism. He began attending Mercer University in 1948, originally planning to become a doctor. After joining the Macon Telegraph, Murphy began to love journalism and reporting. He left Mercer, needing only 20 more credits to graduate, to advance his career in journalism. Later, Mercer named the CCJ after him to commemorate his accomplishments in journalism.

His career as a journalist came with its ups and downs. In 1974, as an editor for the Atlanta Constitution, Murphy was kidnapped by a right-wing activist who was a part of the American Revolutionary Army and was disgusted at the leftist, liberal, news media outlets (and journalists). Murphy was known to write relatively moderate reports but was very much against the Vietnam War.

The kidnapper, William A. H. Williams, asked for $700,000 from the Atlanta Constitution as ransom. Murphy was only able to make this one call while being held hostage. After two days, Murphy was released when the money was delivered to Williams, however, Williams was found not too long after he released Murphy, and was captured by law enforcement.

This story is interesting and powerful, especially for me as a Mercer Student. Although I do not major or minor in journalism, I find myself very close to that department and the students within it. When I first started undergrad in 2021, the journalism and media studies departments and courses were like siblings. Many of the classes were intertwined and both students in journalism and media studies took similar classes.

As found through Mercer’s archived catalogs, it wasn’t till 2024 that the Media Studies department became more separate and rearranged courses to display MFS rather than JMS for all media and journalism courses. I found this change to be important for the growth of the Media Studies department.

But as stated before, these two departments would not be as successful as they are without Reg Murphy rising and accomplishing so much after he departed from Mercer’s journalism program. It must be any journalist's worst fear to have such physical retaliation from a public audience member, especially one that does so in such an extreme way. However, after searching through different articles about the kidnapping, I’ve found that Murphy was strong in his political stance and stayed true to himself even in such a stressful situation. In a New York Times article, Murphy states:

“Does that smell like filthy lucre to you?” (said after receiving ransom money that was stashed in two suitcases) Mr. Murphy said Mr. Williams had asked.

“No, colonel,” Mr. Murphy said. “That smells like freedom to me.”

In journalism, it is important to stand your ground and not sway your opinion to please others, but still report with a reasonable unbiased opinion. As I have taken a couple of journalism classes, I can say that finding a good medium is an important skill to learn as a reporter/journalist. Even as I take this ENG 391 class, Public Writing and the Digital Humanities, and start to write for a more public audience (such as you), I’m learning how important it is to navigate through these digital spaces with clarity and responsibility, as Reg Murphy once did.

As a senior in the Media Studies program, I can see the importance of both journalism and media studies within the broader future of both the university and the world. As digital platforms and polarization rise in the United States, it is important to remember the power of journalism (human journalism, none of that A.I. journalist stuff). Both the Journalism and Media Studies departments excel in teaching analysis of the social (and digital) world. I have personally learned so much about media and the power of different mediums in shaping society. I find this knowledge to be so important in such a growing digital world.

The CCJ recognized that Reg Murphy was remarkable and his story of overcoming such a traumatic event was something to be looked up to by not just Mercer students, but the community as a whole. The commitment he had towards journalism speaks to more than just his resilience but also his character. By contributing back to where he started, Mercer University students are able to have a collaborative space to learn about technology, journalism, society/the public, film, and media, while also paying tribute to such a remarkable student who came before them.

I am excited to see what will come of the Journalism and Media Studies department at Mercer University. The CCJ has always been like a second home to me after so many late nights editing, and countless hours of classes and meetings with other students. Not only is the CCJ a place to learn but also a community full of amazing professors that I’ve grown so close to and such talented students that I get to call my close friends. The Reg Murphy Center of Collaborative Journalism will always represent power in journalism and media thanks to the students, professors, staff, and especially, the extraordinary Reg Murphy.
