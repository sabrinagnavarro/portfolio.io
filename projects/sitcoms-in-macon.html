Sitcoms in Macon

Archival Research Study

This research project explores how 1950s sitcoms shaped local culture in Macon, Georgia during the rise of television. Using archival broadcasts and newspapers, it examines how shows like I Love Lucy reinforced idealized family values and influenced community identity.

Television in the South: The Cultural Reception of 1950s Sitcoms in Macon, Georgia

MFS 405 Final Paper · Dr. Schaff

Introduction

The 1950s saw the rise of television becoming a significant part of daily American life, first starting as a luxury item and soon becoming a staple in every American household. Sitcoms were particularly popular in this early era. Beginning in radio, sitcoms made a dramatic switch to the new audiovisual medium. The family-friendly genre emerged as one of that era's most influential and widely consumed. Sitcoms reflected the social norms and values of postwar America, portraying an idealized family life. In this paper, I will examine the popularity of sitcoms in Macon, Georgia, from 1956 to 1965 by researching how they were broadcast on local television stations like WMAZ and WGXA. I will also look at how shows were promoted and received in Macon by examining local newspapers such as the Macon Telegraph and Macon News. In doing so, I will analyze how sitcoms both influenced and were shaped by Macon's social and political context in the 1950s and 1960s.

Contextual Background

Between 1956 and 1965, America went through significant social and political changes, which were reflected in television shows broadcast. Post-war economic prosperity led many white Americans to flee from urban areas to avoid racial integration to the suburbs, where they would live out the “American Dream,” –driven by the belief that anyone, regardless of their background, can achieve success in America through hard work. However, it is important to note that this “dream” excludes lower-income households along with other marginalized populations. As suburban living became popular, owning a television set became a necessity for many families.

Prime time shows like I Love Lucy, Bewitched, and The Dick Van Dyke Show portrayed traditional family values and idealized middle-class life. The traditional family here describes the nuclear family, which is invariably a heterosexual married couple with kids. The father is often positioned as the breadwinner, with the mother staying home as the caregiver. The middle-class life they depict idealizes the material without excessive wealth. The families live comfortably with food, safety, and education. All of these sitcoms, however, exclude and ignore the struggles of reality, like single-mothers, queer households, and working-class families. Shows like The Andy Griffith Show, set in rural North Carolina, depicted an idealized version of the South, one with no racial tensions.

In the south, and Middle Georgia and Macon, in particular, television served as an affordable source of entertainment for middle to lower class families. As Nancy Hendricks notes, television was “free of charge” once the initial investment in the TV set was made, making it an accessible form of leisure for families in a region with limited economic resources. Hendricks goes on to say, “The daily lives of average Americans generally centered on gathering together in front of the TV to watch programs as a family unit. Many shows were gentle situation comedies that usually came with a moral at the end…” As previously noted, these sitcoms often reinforced social norms, offering an escape while also promoting family-friendly values.

Broadcast Television Stations

At the time, Macon had two local television stations, WMAZ and WGXA. These stations played a large role in bringing national television to Middle Georgia. Both delivered popular prime-time sitcom shows and other programming to the area. Beginning in 1953, WMAZ was a CBS affiliate with additional affiliations with ABC and NBC. WGXA first launched in 1982 as a Fox affiliate and would play sitcoms from then on. By looking through the TV Guides from 1956 to 1965, it becomes clear that WMAZ (channel 13) typically broadcast a variety of sitcoms, westerns, and crime dramas during prime time viewing. Sitcoms include Father Knows Best, Dennis the Menace, The Jack Benny Show, and other popular sitcoms of that era. Again, these sitcoms portrayed middle-class life in suburban settings. For example, Father Knows Best focused on the man as the center of the household, reinforcing the patriarchal status quo.

Advertisements

Throughout the 1950s and 1960s, local newspapers in Macon played a significant role in promoting popular family sitcoms like I Love Lucy and Father Knows Best. Advertisers would promote their products using popular characters from these beloved sitcoms, and the newspaper would publish articles responding to the private lives of the actors who played them. These advertisements and reception articles published in the Macon Telegraph reflect the sitcom's appeal to Macon’s public audience and shed light on their role in shaping local culture. One notable advertisement encouraged audiences to buy products linked to the show I Love Lucy. Ads in the Macon Telegraph promoted the idea of dressing like the star Lucille Ball. These ads, which read “dress like Lucy,” promoted fashion pieces and garments directly targeted at wives and mothers. One 1955 ad linked products like lace tablecloths, storkline beds, and cloth window shades to the I Love Lucy brand. These advertisements seek to sell Lucy’s domestic lifestyle onscreen to the show’s suburban audience, in this case, Macon. Similarly, ads for the Father Knows Best sold products targeted at men. One 1957 Father’s Day ad in the Macon Telegraph promoted hats, shoes, belts, and watching Father Knows Best as perfect gifts for any Father. These ads mirrored the family values promoted on the show, aligning the ideal family life in Father Knows Best with life in Macon.

Highlighted Stories and Reception

The kind of news stories the Macon Telegraph published also tells us about the public reception of these sitcoms in Macon, Georgia. The Macon Telegraph tended to highlight stories using Lucille Ball’s star power and icon status. Articles covered her private life and romance with on-and-off-screen husband Desi Arnaz. These articles covered scandals surrounding Desi Arnaz and a prostitute and Lucy and Desi’s eventual divorce. This divorce of the on-and-off-screen couple must have been tragic to the show’s many longtime fans. The fantasy of the perfect on-screen couple was not the same in the real world, which would have brought many fans back to reality. In 1962, the Macon Telegraph quietly reported a scandal involving Bill Gray, who played Bud on Father Knows Best, who awaited trial for possessing narcotics. The brief report suggests that they wanted to keep the story under the radar so as not to infringe on the illusion of his sitcom character. This story would destroy the wholesome, family-friendly image of the show and would further remind fans that the sitcom is a facade and not reality. The lives of the sitcom stars were important because they brought the made-up into the real world, where not all problems are solved at the end of twenty-two minutes.

Women Hater John Crosby

Other public reception of I Love Lucy included an article by John Crosby titled “Cherchez Les Femmes.” This French title loosely translates to “Women cause the problem,” and Crosby takes a misogynistic and frankly, ignorant, take that women are bad actresses and are box office “poison.”

Crosby states, “And when you come right down to it- I’m laying myself wide open to a punch in the mouth here- did women ever amount to much in the movies? The big box office stars of my childhood were Charles Chaplin, Douglas Fairbanks, Harold Lloyd, Buster Keaton, Rudolph Valentino, Lon Chaney. Any women in there? Well, all right, there was a girl named Mary Pickford. But was Mary Pickford really a woman? The only thing I ever saw her in was ‘Little Lord Fauntleroy,’ and she was anything but a woman in that.”

He lists many famous actresses and female directors and diminishes them by calling them “old ladies” or “kids” who need a big star actor to get people to the box office. In this article, he also mentions Lucille Ball as possibly the biggest television star of all time (note how he did not call her the best). He begins by explaining how television used to be a women’s medium, meaning that television was a low form of entertainment, explicitly not art. He also mentions how “women's shows” have no audience and that “now” (in 1961) shows are all male-centered since television now holds more prestige than when it started. Although I Love Lucy was a highly broadcast and popular show, this Macon media critic’s reception was not enthusiastic about it, or its female star. At the time, many families in Macon tuned into WMAZ to watch sitcoms like I Love Lucy and certainly kept up with the personal lives of the actors. Though this critic decided to use the Macon Telegraph as his platform to uniformly reject women in the industry, it does not count for its popularity in Macon or the national arena.

John Crosby’s article on women in the film industry effectively demonstrates the misogyny in media. In this article, Crosby insults female stars, degrading them based on looks and less on talent. This was not a shocking take during the time since American media perpetuated a domestic portrayal of women that stuck to traditional gender roles. Crosby’s sexist commentary mirrors how dismissive the media was to female stars. These female stars were often sexualized and objectified, which can be seen through how Crosby decided to insult them (their bodies). This sexism extended far beyond media criticism and into industry practices such as women being attached to certain genres, and actresses being paid less than their male counterparts. Even in Macon, where women-led sitcoms were popular, female-led media entertainment was criticized by critics and possibly audiences as well.

Who Was Written About and Who Was Not

Reception for I Love Lucy was starkly different that of Father Knows Best. Although both shows were immensely popular from 1956 to 1965, the only articles I found about the critical success of the shows were from Father Knows Best. For instance, the Macon Telegraph highlighted many of Father Knows Best Emmy wins, including best actress for Jane Wyatt. There was community engagement with these shows in Macon, Georgia; for example, Father Knows Best inspired local events like the Jones County Gardeners’ show, where “television interpretations” included themes from the show. Community engagements like this show that the sitcom was more than just a form of entertainment for the family, but also a topic of conversation outside, in the community.

Conclusion

Examining the broadcast and reception of sitcoms between 1956 and 1965 in Macon, Georgia, it is evident that these programs played an influential role in shaping local culture. This period was marked by significant social and political change post-war, which included the civil rights movement and the rise of television and suburbia. Sitcoms like I Love Lucy and Father Knows Best provided escapism and mirrored the idealized “American Dream.” The WMAZ was important in delivering this programming to Middle Georgia, while the Macon Telegraph was vital to delivering TV Guide information, along with advertisements and public reception of these sitcoms. Advertisements like “dress like Lucy” promoted local products by aligning themselves with the sitcom characters on screen, further blurring the lines between entertainment and consumer culture. Fascination with the actors off-screen shows the connection between the sitcom and the local community. 1950s and 1960s sitcoms reflected the societal values of Macon, Georgia, while also molding their perceptions through targeted marketing and newspaper stories.
